01The problem

Flat line. Three very different stories.

You hired an SEO person or agency. There were targets. The date came, and the numbers didn't.

Real talk: a deadline can't answer that. Google doesn't even promise one. Its own starter guide says some changes take effect in a few hours and others could take several months.

What a flat line can do is hide what's really going on. Click through the three stories below. Same traffic, completely different reality underneath.

Each story calls for a different move: push for execution, give it time, or change strategy. Firing your SEO in story two is as costly as renewing in story three.

So how do you know which story you're in? I started with what the experts measure.

02The research

8 trusted sources. One unanimous answer.

I didn't want another list of 30 metrics. I wanted to know which ones the people who define SEO actually agree on.

So I put Google's documentation for site owners side by side with the KPI guides from Ahrefs, Semrush, Search Engine Land, Search Engine Journal, Seer Interactive, Shopify and HubSpot. Then I counted. Click any bar to see who's in.

8/8

Business results win. Ahrefs calls organic conversions "the only indisputable SEO KPI."

1/8

Only Google talks to you, the buyer. Every other guide is written for SEOs reporting to their boss. That's the gap this guide fills.

Line these metrics up in the order things actually happen, and you get a chain.

03The framework

SEO is a chain. Break one link and everything after it dies.

Work has to go live before Google can see it. Google has to show it before people click. People have to click before they buy.

Try it: click any link below to break it, and watch what happens downstream.

Semrush splits KPIs the same way. Links 1 and 2 are leading indicators: they move early, so you can fix things while there's still time. Link 3 is the lagging indicator: it confirms the result, but it always shows up last.

That's also why a missed deadline is so misleading. It only looks at link 3, the slowest one.

Now let's walk the chain, metric by metric.

Link 1 · Was the work done?

Work delivered and verified

The share of your SEO's recommendations that are actually live on your site. Sounds basic. It's also the metric almost nobody checks.

Here's why it matters: a lot of SEO work never ships. In a 2024 survey of technical SEOs, the most common answer was that only 40–60% of their recommendations got implemented. Often the blocker isn't the SEO. It's a developer with no time, or an approval stuck in someone's inbox, sometimes yours.

Example · a change log that actually helps
RecommendationOwnerStatus
Rewrite titles on 12 service pagesSEOLive, verified
Fix redirects from old product URLsDeveloperBlocked
Add a new location pageYouWaiting approval
Where
Ask for a dated change log. Spot-check a few items on the live site.
Good sign
Key items live; blocked items say why.
Red flag
No log. Just "we did a lot of work."
Ask
"What did you recommend, what's live, and what's blocking the rest?"
Does your SEO report show this?

Sources: Google, Do you need an SEO?; SEOFOMO Technical SEO survey, 2024.

Link 1 · Was the work done?

Indexing of your key pages

If Google hasn't indexed a page, it doesn't exist in search. Period. So before you judge rankings or traffic, make sure your service, product and location pages are actually in.

Google recommends checking Search Console about once a month, including the indexing report and Manual Actions. Skip the site-wide page count; Ahrefs says that mostly matters for big sites. You only care about your money pages.

Where
Search Console → Indexing → Pages
Good sign
All key pages indexed. New ones in within weeks.
Red flag
Money pages "not indexed" for months, no plan.
Ask
"Are all our key pages indexed? What's the plan for the rest?"
Does your SEO report show this?

Sources: Google, Get started with Search Console; Google, Debugging drops in Search traffic; Ahrefs, SEO KPIs.

Link 2 · Did visibility grow?

Non-brand clicks, versus last year

Clicks from searches that don't include your business name. People who type your name already know you. Non-brand clicks are new customers who found you because of SEO. That's the whole point.

Example · same total, different story
Last yr
Brand 600
New 400
This yr
Brand 750
250

Total clicks look flat at 1,000. Underneath, SEO is bringing fewer new people. That's what to ask about.

Always compare with the same months last year, not last month, so seasons don't fool you. And use clicks, not impressions: Google confirmed impressions were misreported from May 2025 to April 2026. Clicks were fine.

Where
Search Console → Performance → Branded filter: non-brand
No filter?
Smaller sites may not have it yet. Filter out your brand name and misspellings instead.
Good sign
Up versus last year, or steady while your market shrank.
Red flag
Only total traffic, and the growth is all brand.
Ask
"How did non-brand clicks change versus last year?"
Does your SEO report show this?

Sources: Ahrefs, Semrush, Search Engine Land, Shopify KPI guides; Google, branded queries filter; Search Console data anomalies.

Link 2 · Did visibility grow?

Search breadth

How many different non-brand searches send you at least one click. When it grows, Google is showing you for more of what your customers look for. And it usually moves before traffic does. Best early signal you've got.

Ahrefs puts search visibility at the top of its KPI list. The catch: SEO tools measure it with keyword lists your SEO picks. Counting real searches in Search Console uses Google's own data. Free, and impossible to cherry-pick.

Where
Search Console → Performance → Queries, non-brand → Export & count
Good sign
More searches over time, matching what you sell.
Red flag
Growth only in searches unrelated to your business.
Ask
"Which new searches are we showing up for?"
Does your SEO report show this?

Sources: Ahrefs, Semrush, Search Engine Journal, Seer, Shopify KPI guides.

Link 2 · Did visibility grow?

Where you rank

Ditch the single "average position" number. Count how many non-brand searches sit in positions 1–3, 4–10 and 11–20. Now you can see searches climbing toward the top.

Top-3 searches nearly doubled; the top 10 grew from 29 to 49. That's real progress, even before traffic catches up.

Average position hides all this. It can even "improve" while you lose visibility: when low-ranking searches drop off, the average goes up. Google says not to obsess over absolute position, and Ahrefs calls single keyword rankings an inferior metric.

Where
Search Console → Performance → Queries + Position → 3 buckets
Good sign
Money searches climbing into the top 10 and top 3.
Red flag
Only average position, or screenshots of hand-picked keywords.
Ask
"How many important searches moved into the top 3?"
Does your SEO report show this?

Sources: Google, Debugging drops in Search traffic; Ahrefs, SEO KPIs; Search Engine Land, SEO KPIs.

Link 2 · Did visibility grow?

Clicks to pages that sell

Traffic isn't revenue. This one only counts non-brand clicks landing on pages that can make money: services, products, categories, locations, pricing.

Search Engine Land lists "pages generating traffic" as a real-world KPI. Eventbrite judged its own SEO by traffic to event listings, the pages that sell tickets. And HubSpot is the cautionary tale: its blog traffic fell sharply, yet its CEO said in 2025 that only about 10% of leads came from the blog. Viral posts don't pay the bills.

Where
Search Console → Performance → Pages, non-brand → Group by type
Good sign
Pages that sell gaining non-brand clicks.
Red flag
All growth on blog posts nobody buys from.
Ask
"How much search traffic lands on pages that sell?"
Does your SEO report show this?

Sources: Search Engine Land, SEO KPIs; HubSpot, blogging KPIs; Google case study, Eventbrite; HubSpot Q2 2025 earnings call.

Link 3 · Did it hit the business?

Verified leads and sales

Calls, forms, bookings and sales from organic search, confirmed in your own records, and compared with what you pay. The one metric all eight sources agree on.

"Confirmed" is doing the heavy lifting here. Some "conversions" are just page views or button clicks, and Google notes that Search Console and Analytics count visits differently. Your CRM settles it. Because this link shows up last, don't make it your only test early on. But it's the final boss.

Where
GA4 → Traffic acquisition → Organic Search → Key events
Then
Match against your CRM, call log or sales records.
Good sign
Organic leads rising, and sales confirms they're real.
Red flag
No key events, or leads your sales team calls junk.
Ask
"How many real leads came from organic, and how do you know?"
Does your SEO report show this?

Sources: Ahrefs, Semrush, Search Engine Land, Search Engine Journal, Seer, Shopify, HubSpot; Google, Using Search Console and Analytics data; GA4 Help, key events.

Link 3 · For local businesses

Google Business Profile actions

Serve a local area? Then plenty of your customers never touch your website. They call or tap "directions" straight from your Google Maps listing. Your profile tracks every one of those actions. Semrush and Shopify both list them as SEO KPIs.

Where
Your Business Profile → Performance
Good sign
Calls, directions and website clicks up versus last year.
Red flag
Never reported, or only "views".
Ask
"How many calls and direction requests came from our profile?"
Does your SEO report show this?

Sources: Google Business Profile Help, performance; Semrush and Shopify KPI guides.

06The proof

12 real businesses. Here's what they measured.

Telecom, marketplaces, local trades, B2B software. The question wasn't how big the wins were. It was which metrics they trusted.

Quick honesty check: not all evidence is equal. Only three of these were proper controlled tests. Filter the table to see who's who.

Put all twelve side by side and four lessons jump out:

  1. Many SEO changes do nothing. Some hurt.

    A Pinterest title test had zero measurable effect. Some Etsy title tests hurt traffic badly. A change log is how you catch the bad ones fast.

  2. The pros measure money, not rankings.

    ZipRecruiter tracked non-branded traffic and conversions. Vodafone tracked leads and sales. Eventbrite tracked the pages that sell tickets.

  3. Traffic isn't the business.

    HubSpot's blog lost most of its traffic, yet it brought in only about a tenth of the company's leads.

  4. Some drops aren't your SEO's fault.

    HouseFresh took about two years to recover from a Google update. Check if your whole market moved before pointing fingers.

You've got the metrics and the proof. Time to diagnose yours.
